META Pre-Earnings Setup: Capex, AI, and the Print

By AI Market Cap 2026-10-21 #META pre-earnings setup

Meta was trending on Yahoo Finance this week, which puts META back into the bucket of AI names traders are actively positioning ahead of its next print. That is the right framing for the stock right now: META is no longer just an ad business with a VR side project. It is one of four hyperscalers running the most aggressive AI infrastructure buildout on the planet, and the print is where that capex story meets the actual revenue line.

This is a look at the pre-earnings setup for META — why the capex number has become the dominant catalyst, what the ad business has to keep delivering to justify it, and how to think about size into a print where guidance usually matters more than the reported quarter.

Why Meta Trades on Capex, Not Just Earnings

The single most important number in any Meta print is the one that lowers reported earnings per share: capital expenditure. Meta has run capex above $30 billion in multiple recent years, and its stated ambition is to keep scaling infrastructure for Llama-family models, generative AI ad creative, and the long-horizon Reality Labs roadmap. That spending hits the income statement as depreciation two to three quarters later, which means today's capex is depressing the EPS line that headlines the print.

The market has been willing to forgive that drag because the AI infrastructure is generating measurable revenue lift. Reels monetization has been a multi-year catch-up story, and AI-driven ad targeting and creative tools have shown up in conversion-rate data inside Meta's earnings calls. As long as management can keep drawing a clean line from a billion dollars of GPU spend to incremental ad revenue, the multiple holds. The risk is when that line gets fuzzy — when the capex number climbs but the ad RPM doesn't follow.

For the pre-earnings trade, the practical question is which line item the market is repricing this cycle: capex, ad growth, or Reality Labs operating losses. In most quarters, only two of those are in active debate. The third is ignored. Pin down which two matter for this print and you have your setup.

What Drives the Reaction

Three disclosures usually carry the print:

  • Capex guidance for the next year. Meta guides capex annually and updates it at each earnings call. A raised guide means management is leaning further into AI infrastructure; a flat guide is read as the first sign of discipline. Historically, Meta's capex step-ups have triggered sell-offs of 5-10% even on in-line earnings, because the stock is priced for free cash flow, not for revenue acceleration.
  • Family Daily Active People (DAP) and ad pricing. User growth is slowing into the high single digits, so the real DAP story is engagement mix and time spent. Ad pricing is the lever that turns time spent into revenue. Watch the price-per-ad disclosure — when that number decelerates, the thesis gets harder to defend even if total ad revenue stays in line.
  • Reality Labs operating losses. Reality Labs has run operating losses above $15 billion in recent years. TheStreet generally writes this off as a long-duration bet on AR/VR. What gets attention is when Reality Labs' losses widen faster than expected without a clear revenue offset. The narrative risk is real even though the dollar impact is small relative to the ad business.

How to Position

The headline EPS number for META is increasingly less useful than the underlying disclosures. Wall Street models EPS to the penny and the reaction is usually a function of guidance, not the reported quarter. That means the pre-earnings trade is really a trade on guidance expectations, not on whether Meta beats by a few cents.

Three rules of thumb into the print:

  • Compare current implied move (using options pricing if available) versus the average 6-8% post-earnings move Meta has delivered in recent quarters. If implied is rich, sell premium into the print rather than buying directional.
  • Watch the capex line in cash flow from investing. If free cash flow year-over-year compresses by more than 15-20%, the multiple compression risk dominates the beat, even on a strong revenue print.
  • Track open interest shifts in the week before the print. A heavy put build-up alongside a flat stock is the classic sign that the buy-side thinks guidance has to disappoint. That same setup has marked the bottom of every META selloff in the last two years.

Actionable Next Steps

The cleanest pre-earnings setup on META this cycle is to anchor the trade to capex guidance, not to the EPS estimate. Decide in advance what level of capex commentary you are willing to own — a reiteration of the prior guide is mildly bullish; an explicit raise above the high end of the consensus range is a momentum buy; any softening is a sell signal regardless of how clean the quarter looks on the surface.

For traders willing to express a directional view without picking the exact print direction, put spreads funded by short call premium have been the dominant pre-earnings structure on META in recent quarters. They collect when the implied move compresses and define both sides of the trade cleanly. Just size to the implied volatility — Meta's pre-earnings IV has historically run 30-50% above its trailing 30-day average, which is enough premium to sell even if the trade doesn't work.

Either way, treat the next print as a capex event with an ad report attached. That mental model is closer to how Meta actually trades than the EPS-beat framework most retail traders still use.
